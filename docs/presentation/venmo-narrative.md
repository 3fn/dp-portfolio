# Venmo Case Study: Tokens as Trust

## The Narrative (for presentation content)

---

### The Situation

When I joined Venmo in July 2019, there was no design system. Not a neglected one. Not a half-built one. Nothing.

What there was: four previous attempts by engineering to install one, all flatly rejected by designers. The designers saw systems as restrictive, too technical, something that would constrain their creativity. The engineers were burned and skeptical of any new attempt. These two organizations were at odds, and I was walking into the middle of it. The design team itself had only recently recovered from feuding internally between its San Francisco and New York offices.

To make it harder: this was pre-COVID, with teams spread across San Francisco, Chicago, and New York working in Sketch. Driving alignment across three timezones with two organizations that didn't trust each other.

---

### The Insight

The source of the problem wasn't technical. It wasn't that nobody knew how to build a design system. It was that trust was broken. Engineering had led previous attempts without design investment. Designers felt things were being imposed on them, not built with them. The result was rejection, four times over.

My approach had to be different. I couldn't lead with components (too visible, too threatening to designer autonomy, too assumptive). I couldn't lead with process (too abstract, no proof of value). I had to lead with something that was shared language, low threat, and something both sides could contribute to and invest in.

Tokens.

---

### The Approach

I started by cultivating a small group of engineers; not the whole org. A handful of people who were curious and willing to try again. I made it easy for them, and took on the bulk of the effort to demonstrate I could deliver on my promises — even 6am meetings from the office, cross-timezone sessions to accommodate their schedules. I brought them into naming conventions and structure decisions early by emphasizing "everything here is a draft until you say it isn't". They shaped the token system with me. It wasn't mine. It was ours.

Then I bounced between design and engineering, translating each organization's concerns to the other. Designers worried about flexibility. I showed them tokens gave them a vocabulary, not a cage. Engineers worried about adoption and platform divergence. I showed them designers were co-creating and willing.

I was assigned a project last minute: a new product launch — the first to ship on a new visual language I'd helped develop. I had no dedicated resources or time for system work, so I strategically leveraged this project as the vehicle for developing the MVP of the token system. Instead of treating it as just another project, I used it to demonstrate what system deliverables look like in practice. Not a pitch deck about the future. Not a collection of instance-per-instance mocks. A framework. Working proof, today, on a real project that both designers and engineers could see. And I did it all with an Excel sheet — accessible to design, engineering, and marketing partners alike.

Simultaneously, practical success was earning visibility. Senior and lead engineers saw it working. They started advocating internally. I leveraged those relationships to get directors, senior directors, and VPs of engineering to notice — and put me front-and-center within their organization. The grassroots support became organizational support. I presented at Engineering all-hands. Engineering leadership began integrating the design system into engineering onboarding. It was no longer one person's initiative — it was becoming institutional.

Then came the moment: Product wanted the output of the new, fancier visual refresh, but they didn't want to invest in making it sustainable. They wanted to ship it and move on.

I communicated with my director who drew a line in the sand: the new designs are off-limits until there are tokens built to support them. No tokens, no new designs.

With that in my back pocket, I laid it out to the PM. I had a vision that was ready. I had engineering backing pre-aligned. The engineers I'd partnered with on naming backed me up: this wouldn't add much overhead and would eventually speed up development. We put Product in a position where the sustainable path was also the fastest path. They agreed.

Four months. July to October 2019. Zero to in-scope.

---

### The Result

The visual refresh scaled sustainably across the product. It wasn't just shipped and forgotten. While it was an MVP, it was built on a foundation that could evolve.

But the bigger story happened later. In August 2019, I had started pushing accessibility work. Specifically color contrast and usability standards. Some senior designers pushed back. I stood firm.

In March 2021, PayPal legal set a mandate: the entire Venmo app must be accessible by the end of the year. Color was the most pervasive offender. My new head of design (2nd director) was mildly freaked out about having to pivot the team to meet the end-of-year deadline.

This was a major blow to our roadmap, or, at least, it would have been if I hadn't started 18 months earlier. The design deliverables were ready — all we had to do was keep working. I was able to meet the deadline and support further accessibility adoption over the course of a few months, well before the deadline. While other teams scrambled, we were already there. 

Furthermore, the partnership I had developed with Marketing those same 18 months prior meant the brand alignment had already shifted, so product and marketing design were 1:1 — resulting in minimal financial expense.

I ran the entire program independently of any PayPal design system support except to align with PayPal's in-house accessibility team.

---

### The Takeaway

Design systems aren't adopted because they're good. They're adopted because people trust them. Trust comes from co-ownership, demonstrated value, and strategic patience.

Tokens were the shared language that rebuilt the relationship between design and engineering at Venmo. Not components. Not process documents. Not executive mandates. A shared vocabulary that both sides helped create.

---

## Key Emotional Beats (for presentation pacing)

1. **Tension**: Four failed attempts. Two orgs at odds. Three timezones. No system.
2. **Insight**: The problem is trust, not technology.
3. **Strategy**: Start small (tokens), build investment (co-ownership), make it frictionless for engineers to say yes.
4. **Proof**: Strategically leveraged an assigned project as the vehicle. Proved it with an Excel sheet.
5. **Climax**: Product resists. Director draws the line. Engineering backs the play. Product agrees.
6. **Institutional adoption**: Engineering all-hands presentation. Integrated into engineering onboarding. No longer one person's initiative.
7. **Payoff**: Accessibility work started 18 months before the mandate. When it came, we were ready.
